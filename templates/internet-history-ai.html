History of the Internet

From packet-switching research to a worldwide network of networks

  • Internet History
  • Web History

Introduction

The Internet is a worldwide system that connects computers and other devices so they can exchange information. Its development began with research into packet switching and experimental networks such as ARPANET. Technologies including TCP/IP, the Domain Name System and high-speed network infrastructure transformed it from a research network into a global communication system.

Key Milestones

1960s - Packet Switching (Source: Engineering and Technology History Wiki)

Paul Baran, Donald Davies and Leonard Kleinrock developed foundational ideas about packet switching. The method divides data into small packets that can travel independently along different routes before being reassembled.

Why it mattered: Packet switching became the fundamental method used to transmit data across the Internet.

1967 - The ARPANET Plan (Source: Internet Society)

Larry Roberts developed and published a plan for ARPANET while working at the United States Advanced Research Projects Agency.

Why it mattered: ARPANET provided a practical foundation for connecting computers at different research institutions.

1969 - The First ARPANET Message (Source: DARPA)

ARPANET began with four computer nodes. On October 29, the first computer-to-computer signal was sent from UCLA to the Stanford Research Institute.

Why it mattered: It demonstrated that distant computers could communicate through a packet-switched network.

1971 - Network Email (Source: Internet Hall of Fame)

Ray Tomlinson developed network email and selected the @ symbol to separate a username from its destination host.

Why it mattered: Email became one of the earliest and most widely used network applications.

1974 - TCP Is Introduced (Source: Engineering and Technology History Wiki)

Vinton Cerf and Robert Kahn published the initial design of the Transmission Control Protocol. Their work allowed independent networks to communicate as an interconnected network of networks.

Why it mattered: TCP established the central concept behind the modern Internet.

1977 - Internetworking Demonstration (Source: Internet Society)

A successful demonstration connected ARPANET, packet-radio and satellite networks using a common protocol.

Why it mattered: It showed that networks with different designs could operate together as one Internet.

1983 - Transition to TCP/IP (Source: Internet Society)

ARPANET hosts completed their transition from the older Network Control Protocol to TCP/IP on January 1.

Why it mattered: A shared protocol suite allowed different networks to communicate and is often considered the beginning of the modern Internet.

1983 - The Domain Name System (Source: Internet Hall of Fame)

Paul Mockapetris designed the Domain Name System, including its distributed architecture, naming hierarchy and query-and-response protocols.

Why it mattered: DNS replaced an increasingly unmanageable host table and made Internet services accessible through memorable names.

1985 - The First Registered .com Domain (Source: Verisign)

Symbolics.com became the first registered second-level domain under .com on March 15.

Why it mattered: It marked an early step toward the commercial use of the Internet.

1986 - NSFNET (Source: National Science Foundation)

The United States National Science Foundation launched NSFNET to connect academic researchers with supercomputer centres.

Why it mattered: NSFNET expanded academic access and became an important high-speed backbone of the developing Internet.

1989 to 1993 - The World Wide Web (Source: CERN)

Tim Berners-Lee proposed the World Wide Web at CERN in 1989, demonstrated the first browser and server by the end of 1990, and CERN released the Web software into the public domain in 1993.

Why it mattered: The Web made information on the Internet much easier to publish, link and access.

1993 - The Mosaic Browser (Source: NCSA)

The National Center for Supercomputing Applications released Mosaic, a graphical browser that displayed text and images together.

Why it mattered: Mosaic helped make Internet information accessible to people outside specialist research communities.

1995 - The Commercial Internet (Source: Merit Network)

The NSFNET backbone service was retired after growing from 217 connected networks in July 1988 to more than 50,000 in April 1995.

Why it mattered: Its retirement marked the transition from a mainly government-supported research network to a commercial, publicly accessible Internet.

1997 - Wi-Fi Standard (Source: IEEE)

The first IEEE 802.11 standard enabled wireless data transmission at up to 2 Mbit/s over the unlicensed 2.4 GHz radio spectrum.

Why it mattered: Wi-Fi allowed devices to access networks without cables and later made household and mobile Internet use much easier.

1998 - ICANN Is Formed (Source: ICANN)

The Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers was established to coordinate important Internet identifiers, including the domain-name system.

Why it mattered: ICANN created a new international structure for coordinating resources needed for one interoperable Internet.

1998 - Google Is Founded (Source: Google)

Google Inc. was established after Sun Microsystems co-founder Andy Bechtolsheim invested in Larry Page and Sergey Brin's project.

Why it mattered: Search engines helped users navigate the rapidly growing amount of information available online.

2001 - Wikipedia (Source: Wikimedia Meta-Wiki)

Wikipedia launched as a collaborative online encyclopedia built by its users.

Why it mattered: It demonstrated how large online communities could create and distribute knowledge together.

2004 - Social Networking Expands (Source: Meta)

Facebook launched and was followed by services that made online communication and content sharing central to everyday Internet use.

Why it mattered: Internet users increasingly became active creators and participants rather than only readers.

2007 - The iPhone (Source: Apple)

Apple introduced the iPhone, combining a mobile phone, media player and Internet communication device with a multi-touch interface.

Why it mattered: It accelerated widespread mobile access to Internet services.

2012 - World IPv6 Launch (Source: Internet Society)

Internet service providers, equipment manufacturers and websites began enabling IPv6 permanently through World IPv6 Launch.

Why it mattered: IPv6 supplied a vastly larger address space as the pool of available IPv4 addresses became insufficient.

Three Key Internet Technologies

Packet Switching
Divides data into small packets that can travel through different routes across a network.
TCP/IP
A suite of communication protocols that allows different networks and devices to communicate with one another.
Domain Name System (DNS)
Translates human-readable domain names into IP addresses, making Internet resources easier to access.

Internet and Web Are Not the Same

The Internet is the global network infrastructure. The World Wide Web is one service that operates on that infrastructure. Email, file transfer and online gaming can also use the Internet without being part of the Web.
